# How To Kill Weeds Without Killing Grass - This Old House

Skip to content

Maintaining a lush, weed-free lawn can be challenging, but with the right approach, you can effectively eliminate weeds without damaging your grass. This comprehensive guide will explore various methods to combat weeds, from natural solutions to synthetic options, and provide tips for maintaining a healthy, weed-resistant lawn.

Understanding Weed Growth and Prevention

[This Old House](https://this-old-house.themaplelane.com/this-old-house/20260822.html)

Weeds are opportunistic plants that quickly take root wherever they find space and sunlight. Their presence often indicates underlying soil or site problems. For example, ground ivy thrives in damp, shady areas where grass struggles to grow. Improving soil drainage through aeration and selectively pruning trees to allow more sunlight can help combat ground ivy.

To prevent weed growth, focus on creating a healthy lawn through proper mowing, watering, and maintenance. Dense, thriving turfgrass naturally suppresses weed growth by outcompeting nuisance plants for water, sunlight, and nutrients. Performing tasks that your lawn would need anyway—mowing at the correct height, aerating when needed, applying treatments like fertilizer or lime—also helps keep it weed-free.

You’ll need to identify the common weeds in your lawn to choose the best removal method. Not all herbicides work on all species of weeds. Lawn weeds generally fall into three main categories. Broadleaf Weeds

Broadleaf weeds are characterized by wide, flat leaves and are often the easiest to spot in a lawn. Common examples include the following: Dandelions Clover Plantain Chickweed

These weeds often have deep taproots, making them challenging to remove completely. Fortunately, broadleaf weeds are fairly easy to target with herbicide. Grassy Weeds

As the name implies, grassy weeds resemble grass and can be more difficult to identify. They may have round or flat stems, but their leaves are narrow and arranged in pairs. Here are some common grassy weeds: Crabgrass Annual bluegrass Quackgrass Foxtail

These weeds often spread quickly and can be particularly difficult to remove in lawns, since herbicides that kill them usually also kill turfgrass.

Pro Tip: A single crabgrass seed can produce a clump that spreads out and smothers existing grass. The best way to combat crabgrass is to maintain a thick, healthy lawn cut at 2.5 to 3 inches tall. This creates a shady condition at ground level that prevents crabgrass seeds from germinating—crabgrass cannot take shade at all. Sedge Weeds

Sedge weeds also have a somewhat grassy appearance, but their leaves are arranged in sets of three and their stems are triangular. They reproduce by sending out runners below the surface of the soil rather than by seeds, and they can be identified by their tall, flowered stems. Here are some common sedge weeds: Yellow nutsedge Purple nutsedge Smallflower umbrella sedge Green kyllinga

Their underground runners, some of which have small tubers attached, make sedge weeds difficult to remove by hand. Natural Weed Control Methods

Although synthetic herbicides work quickly, not all homeowners find them to be the best method of weed control. These herbicides can be toxic to animals and pollinating insects, and they pollute runoff. Try the following weed control methods before reaching for synthetic chemicals. Hand-Weeding iStock

Hand-weeding is one of the most effective methods for controlling weeds in small lawns or when dealing with isolated weed patches. It’s most effective against annual broadleaf weeds and should be done when the soil is moist. If you weed by hand, wear gloves and remember to pull out as much of the root system as possible. Once removed, smooth the soil and add compost to the area. If necessary, patch the spot with lawn seed and keep it evenly moist until the grass reaches 1 inch high.

For perennial weeds like dandelions, you must remove the entire root to prevent regrowth. This also means bagging and disposing of pulled weeds rather than allowing them to decay on your lawn. Spreading Mulch

Mulching can effectively suppress weed growth, especially in garden beds and around trees where turfgrass is unwanted or unlikely to grow. A layer of organic mulch can block sunlight from reaching weed seeds, preventing germination. Additionally, mulch helps retain soil moisture and improve soil health, benefiting your desired plants. Landscape fabric can provide a similar solution for garden beds. Note that you’ll need to reapply mulch about once a year to ensure there’s a sufficient layer for weed prevention. Applying Corn Gluten Meal Courtesy SaferBrand

Corn gluten meal is a natural pre-emergent herbicide that can prevent weed seeds from putting down roots. It’s particularly effective against annual weeds like crabgrass, but to be effective, it must be spread at the right time. Apply corn gluten meal in early spring after weed seeds have sprouted but before they’ve grown roots. Corn gluten-based products like Concern Weed Prevention Plus stop weeds before they start by preventing them from taking root. Water well after application, then allow the area to dry out until the sprouted weeds have died.

Propane-fueled torches called flame weeders are another option for incinerating existing weeds without resorting to synthetic herbicides. They’re more often used in large-scale commercial agriculture, but homeowners can also purchase flame weeders. These must be used with extreme caution, since they will kill whatever the flame comes in contact with. To avoid starting a fire, never use a flame weeder around mulch or dry foliage. These are probably most useful to homeowners with large concrete or paved areas where weeds grow through the cracks. Homemade Solutions

A quick internet search will turn up all kinds of homemade “natural” or “organic” herbicides, though they are often difficult to use and have widely varying results. For instance, some people swear by pouring boiling water on weeds, which will often kill the weed—as well as any other plant it might touch. As with flame weeding, this solution is most useful for weeds in cracks in driveways and sidewalks.

Other common solutions include ingredients like vinegar, borax, or essential oils. These are often less toxic to humans, but they aren’t selective about the plants they kill, and they may still be harmful to some animals and insects. They also typically take a long time and many applications to work.

If your goal is environmentally friendly weed control, we recommend an integrated approach. This could combine hand-weeding with organic herbicides like corn gluten meal, as well as compost fertilization and a proper lawn maintenance schedule to create a healthy lawn that is less friendly to weeds. Synthetic Weed Control Options

When natural methods aren’t sufficient, synthetic herbicides can be an effective last resort. Use these products carefully and follow the included instructions to avoid harming your lawn or other plants.

Herbicides fall into two categories depending on how and when they work. Pre-emergent herbicides should be applied before weeds grow, but post-emergent herbicides can be used directly on existing weeds. These chemicals can also be selective and targeted toward certain types of plants or non-selective and useful against many or all plants. Pre-Emergent Herbicides

Pre-emergent herbicides prevent weed seeds from germinating or disrupting the early development of the plant. They’re particularly effective against annual weeds like crabgrass, and they won’t harm established plants. Apply these products in early spring before weed seeds sprout, because they won’t be effective against weeds that have already grown.

Synthetic pre-emergents like prodiamine and dithiopyr are available under several brand names. Be cautious when using pre-emergents, as they are non-selective and will also prevent desirable grass seeds from germinating. Post-Emergent Herbicides

Post-emergent herbicides target existing, actively growing weeds. They come in two main types:

Contact herbicides: Kill only the parts of the plant they touch and work quickly against annual weeds

Systemic herbicides: Circulate through the entire plant, killing it completely—they’re more effective against perennial weeds but may require repeated treatments

When choosing a post-emergent herbicide, opt for selective products that target specific weeds without harming your grass. For instance, many broadleaf herbicides are effective against broadleaf weeds but won’t harm grass. However, you should still spot-treat weeds rather than spreading an herbicide over your entire lawn. Weed and Feed Products

Weed and feed products combine fertilizer and herbicides to tackle two tasks at once. While convenient, they can pose risks if the timing for weed control doesn’t align with optimal fertilizing schedules. Additionally, synthetic herbicides are best used as targeted treatments, not spread across the entire lawn as fertilizer often is. If you choose to use this approach, we recommend safer options like corn gluten with added organic fertilizer. Tools for Effective Weed Removal

Prevention is key in the battle against weeds. A healthy, well-maintained lawn naturally resists weed invasion. Here are some strategies to keep your lawn weed-free: Proper Mowing Practices

Many homeowners are tempted to mow their lawns too short so they don’t have to mow as often. However, taller grass shades the soil, preventing weed seeds from germinating. Therefore, we recommend setting your lawnmower blades to the proper height for your grass type. Generally, keep cool-season grasses at 2.5—4 inches and warm-season grasses at 1—3 inches. You should let grass grow longer when temperatures are at their highest.

Also, keeping your mower blades sharp ensures that grass gets cut cleanly. Dull blades damage the grass, causing stress and making it more susceptible to disease and other problems. Check your mower blades for sharpness at least once a year during a spring tuneup. Watering Strategies

Your lawn watering schedule will depend on your climate and species of grass, but there are some general principles that are good to follow. Water deeply but infrequently to encourage deep root growth in your grass. This helps your lawn outcompete weeds for water and nutrients. Aim for about 1 inch of water per week, either from rainfall or irrigation. Soil Health Management

Healthy soil promotes a healthy lawn that can resist weeds. Regularly test your soil and treat it as needed to maintain proper pH and nutrient levels. Dealing With Stubborn Perennial Weeds

Perennial weeds like dandelions and thistle can be particularly challenging due to their deep root systems. The tops of the plants die off each year, but they return from the same roots. Additionally, there are few selective herbicides that target perennial grassy weeds—even effective herbicides may take several years to fully work. Follow these steps for the best results:

Dig out the entire root system, going as deep as possible.

If regrowth occurs, repeat the process to exhaust the plant’s energy reserves.

Consider spot-treating with a selective herbicide if manual removal is ineffective.

Improve the overall health of your lawn to outcompete these weeds. Addressing Specific Perennials

Certain perennial weeds may require specialized approaches. For instance, nutsedge, a common perennial weed, thrives in poorly drained soils. Improving drainage and using specific post-emergent herbicides can help manage nutsedge. Similarly, bindweed, with its extensive root system, may necessitate repeated digging and herbicide application for complete eradication. Identify the stubborn weed species in your yard and determine what conditions cause it to flourish so you can correct them. Biocontrol Agents

Biocontrol agents, such as insects that specifically target weed species, are another innovative method for managing perennial weeds. For example, using leaf beetles to control leafy spurge or employing weevils against invasive thistle species can reduce weed populations in a natural, sustainable manner. There may not be a practical biocontrol agent for every perennial weed, but in some circumstances, they can be highly useful. Preventing Weed Regrowth

After successfully removing weeds, take steps to prevent their return:

Reseed bare spots promptly to prevent new weeds from establishing.

Apply a pre-emergent herbicide in early spring to prevent annual weed seeds from germinating.

Maintain proper lawn care practices, including regular mowing, appropriate watering, and annual fertilization.

Consider adding landscape fabric or mulch in garden beds to suppress weed growth. Regular Monitoring and Maintenance

Frequent lawn inspections can help detect and address weed problems early. Regular maintenance routines, such as fertilization and pest control, contribute to a robust lawn that can resist weed invasions. Aeration and dethatching, performed as needed, ensure optimal soil health and discourage weed establishment. Community and Environmental Practices

When possible, take a long-term approach to weed control. Incorporating native plants and cultivating biodiversity within your lawn and garden can naturally suppress weeds. Native species are well-adapted to local conditions and can outcompete invasive weeds. Encouraging beneficial insects and wildlife can also help control certain weed species, promoting a balanced ecosystem. Our Conclusion

Achieving a weed-free lawn requires a combination of prevention, proper lawn care, and targeted removal techniques. Remember that a healthy, dense lawn is your best defense against weeds.
